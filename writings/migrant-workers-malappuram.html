Working Conditions and Informality Among Migrant Construction Workers in Malappuram District, Kerala

Migrant construction workers form a major part of the labour force in Malappuram district, where ongoing construction activity depends heavily on migrant labour. Many workers come from states such as West Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha in search of better wages and steady employment. Migrant workers have become essential to labour-intensive sectors like construction in Kerala.

The nature of work in this sector is physically demanding and largely informal. Workers are engaged as masons, helpers, carpenters, electricians, and plumbers. In Malappuram, most workers are hired through contractors as daily wage labourers rather than through formal employment systems. Their tasks include carrying materials, mixing cement, bricklaying, plastering, and working at heights. Construction sites are temporary workplaces and often lack basic facilities such as proper shelter, toilets, and safety equipment. Workers typically work around 8–9 hours a day and get work for most days in a month, making the job tiring and risky.

The working conditions of migrant construction workers in Malappuram are mostly informal and insecure. Workers usually do not have written contracts, paid leave, or job security. Access to social security benefits such as health insurance and pensions is also limited. A key issue is contractor-based exploitation, which is commonly observed in the district. Workers depend on contractors for wages, leading to delayed payments, partial payments, or non-payment. In many cases, only part of the wage is paid initially, and the remaining amount is delayed, forcing workers to continue working to recover their dues.

Housing conditions are also poor. Migrant workers in Malappuram often stay in small rented rooms near construction sites. Many of these rooms are overcrowded, with several workers sharing a single space and sleeping on the floor after long working hours. Rent is often charged on a per-person basis, increasing costs while keeping workers in unhealthy living conditions. Basic facilities such as sanitation and clean water are often inadequate.

Workers also face everyday exploitation, such as being charged extra by private bus conductors for carrying construction tools during travel. Social factors further affect their lives. In Malappuram, many workers face language barriers, as only a small proportion can speak Malayalam, which limits communication and access to services. Their level of integration into local society remains moderate, indicating partial inclusion.

The informal work framework is useful in analysing these conditions. Migrant construction workers in Malappuram clearly fall within informal employment; they lack contracts, have unstable incomes, and limited access to welfare. Workers in such sectors have weak bargaining power and are often excluded from effective labour protection. However, this framework does not fully capture migration motivations, as higher wages and steady employment continue to attract workers.

To improve conditions, three measures are important. First, stricter regulation of contractors is needed to prevent wage delays and partial payments. Second, proper housing with basic facilities and limits on overcrowding should be ensured. Third, access to social security schemes must be improved through better awareness and easier registration.
