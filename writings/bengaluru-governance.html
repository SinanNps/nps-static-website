Urban Governance in Bengaluru: Evaluating the Greater Bengaluru Authority

Bengaluru, a city, is one of the main drivers of economic growth for the entire country. For Karnataka, the city accounts for 40% of its economy and nearly a fourth of the population. Bengaluru’s fast growth to a global tech hub has put remarkable pressure on its urban institutions, infrastructures, and service delivery systems. Bengaluru’s urban growth has overtaken local governments in planning, regulation, and effective development.

The governance of Bengaluru has adapted in response to change in urban scales instead of forward looking institutional design. Established as a colonial municipal body focused on public health and regulations, the city’s early administrative systems were not intended to support democratic principles. The creation of the Bangalore City Corporation in 1949 represented post-independence consolidation, but administrative power remained limited. Opening up of the economy in the 1990s reshaped Bengaluru into a global city, while local governments lagged behind with quick urban sprawl and population growth. The creation of the Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike in 2007 increased its governing area but created more complications of coordination and accountability, mainly due to the increased influence of parastatal agencies. Subsequent revisions, including the BBMP Act, 2020, tried restricted devolution of power through ward level mechanisms. Most recently, the Greater Bengaluru Governance Act, 2024 reflects official recognition that Bengaluru requires metropolitan scale governance, even as debates continue over centralisation, democratic control, and effective local empowerment.

The Greater Bengaluru Governance Act, 2024, which creates the Greater Bengaluru Authority (GBA) as the city’s highest-level urban institution, represents a significant restructuring of Bengaluru’s municipal governance framework. The Act abolishes the existing Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike (BBMP) and restructures the metropolitan region into several city corporations, all of which fall under the control of the GBA, which is assigned responsibilities for planning, infrastructure, and regulatory oversight throughout the area. The legislation has been criticized as both lacking seriousness and being impossible to implement, and assessments by civil society suggest that the Act fares badly on key aspects, including political representation, transparency, accountability, and citizen participation. By vesting substantial authority in the state government, particularly through the Chief Minister’s role as chairperson of the GBA, the Act is seen as weakening the principle of local self-governance. As a result, while the GBA may unify institutional structures, it risks reinforcing existing governance deficits unless accompanied by genuine decentralisation, safeguards for local democracy, and meaningful mechanisms for public participation.

The Greater Bengaluru Governance Act, 2024 has been widely criticised for centralising power at the metropolitan level while failing to address the underlying weaknesses of urban governance in the city. Janaagraha’s City-Systems assessment shows that the Act scores poorly on urban planning, political empowerment and transparency, with no mandate for integrated three-tier planning and weak provisions for empowered mayors, ward committees and civic participation. Policy analysis by Janaagraha further argues that without stronger devolution of administrative and financial powers to municipal corporations and ward committees, the new structure may simply reproduce existing governance deficits. An ORF brief similarly notes that although the Act is presented as a decentralisation reform, its institutional design is likely to centralise decision-making in the Greater Bengaluru Authority. Concerns about process have also been raised: BBMP held only a three-day public consultation at five locations across the city, and citizen groups have described this as a token exercise that fell short of the thirty-day norm and lacked transparency about how inputs were used. Subsequently, citizen forums have approached the Karnataka High Court, arguing that several provisions of the Act are unconstitutional and undermine local self-governance. Taken together, these critiques suggest the need for amendments that embed genuine metropolitan, municipal and ward-level planning, real fiscal and administrative autonomy for local bodies, robust disclosure and grievance mechanisms, and meaningful, well-publicised public consultations as recommended in civil-society roadmaps.

The creation of the Greater Bengaluru Authority is an important step that recognises the growing size and complexity of Bengaluru. However, studies and civil society assessments show that the Greater Bengaluru Governance Act, 2024 focuses more on changing administrative structures than on improving how the city is actually governed. The Act provides limited support for integrated planning, gives inadequate powers to elected local bodies, and lacks strong systems for transparency, accountability, and citizen participation. To be effective, the GBA needs reforms that strengthen planning at all levels, build institutional capacity, and ensure greater involvement of citizens and local representatives in decision-making. Without these changes, the new governance system may continue the same problems that have affected Bengaluru’s urban management in the past.
